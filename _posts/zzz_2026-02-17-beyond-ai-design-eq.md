**What differentiates a person, a product, or a company in 2026?**

It is not AI.

AI is a **force multiplier**. In capable hands it reshapes organized work, just as earlier multipliers did, Roman road engineering, the medieval heavy plow and stirrup, the Renaissance printing press, the Industrial Age steam engine, the Modern Age adoption of electricity, and the Information Age transistor.

Human society existed before AI and will exist after it. The impact is practical, not apocalyptic. AI amplifies what already exists. A force multiplier allows the same number of people to produce greater outcomes by expanding effective power, productivity, and coordination.

**The differentiator is not the technology. It is the human using it.**

AI increases output, but it does not improve judgment. It does not understand context, intention, or consequence. Those come from people. Two human capabilities therefore become decisive, design thinking, and emotional intelligence.

Good design determines whether a system can actually be used. As Don Norman explains, systems fail not because users are incompetent, but because the design does not communicate how the system works. AI systems are especially vulnerable to this. Early computing captured the same lesson in the phrase “garbage in, garbage out.” When assumptions, data quality, and process safeguards are weak, the output may be precise yet wrong.

AI and ML systems do not understand truth. They detect patterns and predict outcomes. Biased, incomplete, mislabeled, or contextually incorrect data produces confident errors. A large portion of my career has involved troubleshooting technology implementations where the real problem was not the software but the gap between system behavior and human expectation.

An organization may deploy powerful models, yet if outcomes confuse users, especially users who question results, trust does not form. This becomes even more critical when agentic systems operate autonomously without human judgment. At that point the value collapses. The problem is not the model. The problem is the human experience around the model.

A positive side effect of the current AI investment cycle is renewed attention to design. Agents, agentic workflows, MCP, and even the return of the CLI increase transparency. They reveal the actions a system actually allows and the clues showing how to interact with it. In many ways we are moving beyond the limitations of the purely mouse-driven UI toward interfaces that better express system capability. Don Norman’s _The Design of Everyday Things_ influenced me years ago, and it remains highly relevant for anyone building products or technology.

Design, however, is not sufficient by itself. The critical element is still the human who connects other humans to interactive systems. Not the IQ of model builders, but the EQ of the people guiding how AI is used as a multiplier.

Emotional intelligence is the ability to recognize and manage your own emotions, and to understand and respond appropriately to others. In management consulting the name Peter Drucker eventually appears, often associated with the idea that what gets measured gets improved. That applies to humans as well as machines.

Earlier this year I completed an EQ-i leadership assessment and coaching process with Parul Agarwal of Oralume. One of the surprising outcomes for me was not a weakness in problem solving or stress tolerance. I scored strongly in both. What the report highlighted instead was a tradeoff. When I rely heavily on that problem-solving mode, my patience for explanation can diminish.

After all, how many times do I need to explain that water is wet.

The value of the measurement was awareness. The metric itself becomes a tool. It helps me recognize when I need to slow down, communicate differently, and meet the listener where they are. In an interesting parallel, we often ask AI systems to verify their own work. The assessment functions similarly for people. It introduces a deliberate pause between reaction and response.

Those are exactly the abilities still required when working with AI. AI produces probabilities. A person must judge whether they make sense, explain them to others, and accept responsibility for acting on them.

Human-centric technology therefore is not technology that replaces people. It is technology designed around how people think, decide, trust, and collaborate. AI multiplies capability. Design makes it usable. Emotional intelligence makes it responsible.

Organizations that combine all three will outperform those that rely on AI alone.  The value of AI is not in what it produces, but in what people can responsibly do with it together. That is the tapestry we are actually trying to build.
