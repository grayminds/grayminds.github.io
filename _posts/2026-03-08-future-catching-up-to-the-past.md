I have files from the golden age of home computers. Documents written in PFS First Choice, Microsoft Works, WordPerfect, early Microsoft Word, and CorelDraw. Formats from applications I had completely forgotten existed. I haven't heard the name "Microsoft Works" in probably fifteen years.

These files survived every platform transition because of being organized, copying forward as one platform was discarded to the next. They outlasted the applications that created them. They outlasted the operating systems those applications ran on. They persisted, unopenable, through every transition.

And then the future showed up and read them.

## The BBS Files

The oldest batch was the one I least expected to recover: ANSI animation files from bulletin board systems in the early '90s.

In the pre-internet days, there were BBSes (Bulletin Board Systems). All the dial tones of the modem to dial up local numbers, back in the days of long-distance calling charges. One of my early technical hobbies was drawing ANSI animation files for BBSes, both mine and others. Hand-drawn character by character in TheDraw, animated frame by frame, these home screens greeted every caller who dialed in. I collaborated with friends who ran boards, and the home screen was everything.

I pointed Claude Code at the files. "These are ANSI animation files from TheDraw. Convert them to animated gifs."

Claude identified the TheDraw format, parsed the ANSI escape codes for color and positioning, reconstructed the frame sequences, and converted them into animated GIFs. The color palette and frame timing were preserved. Screens I had not seen in over thirty years, rendered on a modern display. Old screen handles I had completely forgotten stared back at me. One was unmistakably a Douglas Adams reference. I must have been deep into _The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy_ that year. I don't remember choosing that handle. But the file does.

## The Documents Nobody Can Open

After the BBS files, I turned to writing samples. High school essays. Undergraduate papers. Graduate coursework. Files spanning a decade of education, saved in formats that no modern application will touch.

The traditional recovery path is a project in itself. Hunt for conversion utilities. Hope they still exist. Upload personal documents to some random website offering free format conversion. Repeat for every file, every format, every era of software.

Instead, I gave Claude Code the folder. "Convert these to markdown." Okay, it was quite a bit more complicated than that.

It identified the formats and converted the content. PFS First Choice, a DOS-era integrated suite, became clean markdown. WordPerfect documents with their proprietary formatting codes became structured text. Microsoft Works files came through intact. No hunting for converters. No uploading to unknown services.

But the actual process had its complications. Some files had Microsoft Equation Editor, which Microsoft locked down due to security issues; all attempts at conversion produced unacceptable results. Other files came through as sentence, line break, sentence rather than paragraph format, which required additional Claude Code instructions to correct. Claude Code also handled building the frontmatter details for long-term reference.

## What the Old Files Show

The recovered documents are a timeline. Over 2,500 markdown files, organized by era and institution. High school writing is earnest and overwrought. Undergraduate work shows the first attempts at structured argument. Graduate writing is tighter, shaped by professors who would not tolerate filler.

None of it needs to be published. But it belongs in a system where I can find it and connect it to what came after. Converting to markdown and filing it in my vault means these documents are no longer trapped in amber. They are part of the personal experience.

That matters more than nostalgia. The patterns are visible. You can trace a line from a college essay about human-computer interaction to a career spent building systems at that exact intersection.

## The Future Reflects the Past

The future caught up to the present to reflect the past.

The tools I have today can read files I created before those tools were imaginable. The AI that converts a PFS First Choice document into markdown was not conceivable when that document was written.

<span class="concept-highlight">The part of AI that rarely gets discussed is not what it builds forward, but what it recovers from behind.</span>

Every person accumulates a digital past. Files in dead formats. Creative work locked inside applications that no longer run. That accumulation used to represent permanent loss, measured in format obsolescence.

It doesn't have to anymore. The future is not just catching up to the present. It is reaching back, reading what was left behind, and making it whole again.
