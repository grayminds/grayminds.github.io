Errors of Omission and Commission

Created on June 28, 2026

2026   ·   claude-code   ai-workflow   mastery   self-assessment   ·   skill-development

When you light a stage, the spotlight is the easy decision. You point it at the one thing the audience cannot miss, and the rest of the stage goes black. That is the whole trick. A spotlight is not a tool for showing. It is a tool for hiding.

I spent years around stage lighting before I spent years around automation, and the two turned out to be the same craft in different clothes. Both come down to where you decide to put the light.

The drive that narrows

Building a skill runs on the same instinct. You pick a target. Maybe it is a certification, a feature you want to ship, or a platform you want to master. You point everything at it. You drive. And the harder you drive, the darker everything outside the beam becomes … not because it stopped mattering, but because you stopped looking.

That focus is a good thing. It is also a narrowing thing. Both are true at once, and the second one is the part nobody warns you about.

Two ways it goes wrong

There is an old pair of terms for how this breaks. Errors of commission, and errors of omission.

An error of commission is something you did. A wrong turn, a bad call, or a bug you wrote. These are loud. The build breaks. The test goes red. The client sends the email. You see them, you feel them, and you fix them. Commission errors arrive with their own alarm bells.

An error of omission is something you never did. The adjacent skill you walked right past. The neighboring tool you never picked up because it sat six inches outside the spotlight. These are silent. Nothing breaks. No alarm. You cannot fix what you never noticed you skipped.

That is the trap. We spend our review energy on commission errors, because they are the ones making noise. The omission errors just sit in the dark and compound.

Stop at the junction

So here is the discipline I keep relearning. When you reach a milestone, stop at the junction before you sprint to the next one. Better companies also call this an after-action review.

By the time you hit a target, you have spent days or weeks with a focused spotlight with the rest of the room in the dark. The milestone is the moment to ask the uncomfortable question: what did I miss while I was heads-down? And then the harder follow-up: what is sitting one step to the side of what I just learned, close enough to grab, that I never even saw?

A map I did not use to have

For most of my career, that question had no good answer and required extra time to track and manage. You could not audit your own blind spots, because by definition they were in the dark. So you guessed. You waited for a colleague to mention something. Sometimes you found out in an interview.

That has changed. Working inside Claude Code, Codex, and local models through LM Studio and Ollama, I now have something I never had before: a running read on my own practice. Not a grade. A map. These tools can show me where I am already strong and, more usefully, where the work I am already doing brushes up against a skill I have never formally claimed. The thing I built last week turned out to be three steps from a capability I did not know was that close.

This is the move from a spotlight, to a high-intensity area light, to plain overhead lighting.

The spotlight shows you the goal. The area light shows you the close neighbors, the skills sitting just past the one you were working on. The overhead light shows you the whole room, the territory you are actually operating in, most of which you were never pointed at. Widening the beam does not change the stage. It changes how much of it you can see.

Here is the same method turned on my own Claude Code practice. Each ring is a week. The inner shape is where I started: bright in a few places, deliberately dark in others. The outer ring is where a handful of small, intentional builds carry it. Read it the right way. The point is not the starting mark on any one spoke. The point is that I can see the whole shape now, and decide where to widen the beam next.

What the floodlight is not

Now, a word about what this is not.

It would be easy to read a map like this as a list of gaps. A confession. “Here are the things Michael cannot do.” That reading is wrong, and it misses the entire point.

A reading on a skill you never aimed at is not a deficiency. It is a choice you can finally see well enough to make on purpose. Expertise has never been about the size of the lit circle. Real expertise includes knowing the shape of your own knowledge, edges and all. The professional to worry about is not the one who can name three areas they want to grow into. It is the one whose spotlight has convinced them that the lit circle is the entire stage.

I know my craft. I have spent decades in intelligent document processing, in automation, and in the work I am known for. What the floodlight gives me is not doubt about any of that. It gives me the next honest step. In a field that reshapes itself every few months, the ability to see your own edges and move toward them on purpose is not a weakness on the resume. It is the most durable thing on it.

Technology is rapidly changing - only the adaptable can deliver now and in the future.

House lights

The spotlight will always be the easy decision. It is also the one that keeps the most of the stage in the dark.

So every so often, I bring up the house lights and look at the whole room. Not because the spotlight was wrong … but because it was never the whole show.
